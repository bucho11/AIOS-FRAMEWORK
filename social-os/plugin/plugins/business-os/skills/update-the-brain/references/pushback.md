# Pushback — reading it, and what to do in the first thirty seconds

Read this before replying to anything that is not a straightforward request.

Pushback is not an interruption to get past. It is the highest-quality signal this
system ever receives: **a document is wrong, and she is telling you where.** Handled
well, the system gets permanently better. Handled with an apology, the same
complaint comes back next month — and by then there are two versions of the truth.

---

## What counts

Five kinds. You are matching intent, not exact words.

**1 · Correction** — a fact or behaviour is wrong.
> "That's wrong." · "No, we don't do that." · "I never said that." · "That's not how
> we say it." · "We stopped offering that."

**2 · Confusion** — she cannot see why something happened.
> "Why did you post that?" · "Where did that come from?" · "I don't understand why
> it's doing this." · "Who told you that?"

Confusion is a correction she hasn't finished forming. Treat it the same way: find
the document, tell her which one it was. Very often that answer *is* the fix, because
she then says "oh — change that."

**3 · Repetition — the most serious.**
> "I already told you." · "We talked about this." · "Again?" · "This keeps
> happening."

**A repeat means a previous fix never landed in a document.** Somebody said "got it"
and changed nothing, or added a note beside the old version instead of replacing it.
So a repeat gets the *full* pass, not a targeted one: check every rule document that
could plausibly govern this, specifically looking for a second document holding the
same role. That is almost always what it is.

Never treat a repeat as her being impatient. It is the system's failure, and saying
so out loud is the right move: *"You did tell me, and it didn't stick — let me find
why."*

**4 · Disagreement or frustration** — she doesn't like where this is going.
> "This isn't what I wanted." · "Stop doing that." · "I don't like these." · "This
> feels off."

Vaguer, and the temptation is to guess and regenerate. Don't. One question.

**5 · Silent rejection** — the one with no words.

She rejects three drafts in a row without saying why. She stops approving. She goes
quiet after being active. Nothing has been said, but something is wrong, and waiting
for her to articulate it is how a client drifts away instead of complaining.

Say it plainly: *"That's three you've passed on — is it the voice, the topic, or the
timing? One word is enough."*

---

## The first thirty seconds

**Do not apologise first.** "Sorry, I'll fix that" is the response that created the
problem this whole system is built against: it sounds like a fix, it changes nothing,
and it ends the conversation before the cause is found. If an acknowledgement feels
needed, acknowledge **once**, briefly, and spend the rest of the sentence on the
cause.

**Do not fix the behaviour without fixing the document.** Adjusting for the rest of
the conversation is the trap. It looks like success today and is gone tomorrow.

**Do not add a note.** Writing "she prefers no exclamation points" somewhere new,
while `Brand voice` still says to use them, creates exactly the two-contradicting-
documents state everything here exists to prevent.

**Do stop what you were doing.** If a rule that governs the current task is in
dispute, the task is on hold. Producing more work under a disputed rule just makes
more work to redo. A dispute about an unrelated room does not stop unrelated work.

Instead, in order:

1. **Name the cause.** *"That came from Brand voice — it says short sentences, no
   emoji."* This one sentence does four things: it proves you found the source, it
   shows her the system is legible, it lets her correct your diagnosis if you got the
   wrong document, and it makes the fix obvious.
2. **If you can't name it, ask one question.** Short, concrete, answerable in a word.
3. **Run the pass** — `SKILL.md`, steps 1–9.
4. **Confirm in one or two lines** — what changed, and what you redid because of it.
5. **Go back to the task**, from the corrected state.

---

## Asking the one question

The goal is a question she can answer while walking. Never make her do the
diagnosis.

| Don't ask | Ask |
|---|---|
| "Could you clarify what you'd like changed?" | "Is it the voice, or the topic?" |
| "How would you like me to handle this?" | "Should that be $25 or $30?" |
| "What are your preferences around tone?" | "Too formal, or too casual?" |
| "Can you tell me more about what felt off?" | "The wording, or the photo?" |

Offer two or three options drawn from what you actually suspect. A question with
options is a question she answers in three seconds. An open question is homework.

**One question, then act.** If the answer is still unclear, make the smallest safe
change, say exactly what you changed, and let the next correction narrow it. Three
questions in a row is an interrogation, and she will stop correcting you — which is
the worst possible outcome, because then the documents quietly rot while everything
looks fine.

---

## When she is wrong

It happens — a misremembered price, a detail that changed and she forgot. Do not
silently flip the document to match, and do not argue.

Say what the document currently holds, in one line, and let her decide:

> "Right now **What we offer** says $25/hour, dated March. Change it to $30?"

One sentence, no defensiveness, and the decision stays hers. Quietly rewriting the
canon to match a misremembering is the same corruption as ignoring her — it just
looks more obedient.

---

## After it is fixed

**Do not keep bringing it up.** One confirmation, then it is done and the corrected
state is simply how things are. Re-litigating a correction, or apologising for it
again later, tells her the system is still uncertain — which is exactly what makes
someone stop trusting it.

The correction lives in the document. The document is the memory. That is the
entire point.
